How the calculation works

Frustration,
three ways

Three tools ask the same question of a protein — is this contact the best one that could sit here? — and they all answer it with the same Z‑score — a count of standard deviations away from a random baseline. Everything that separates them is hidden inside two symbols.

Every number on this page is computed from runs on disk in this repository: ubiquitin, 200 decoys, scored with Rosetta REF2015 (Rosetta's current all-atom energy function), against frustratometeR on the identical input file.

The idea in plain terms

Most of a protein is boring. The interesting parts are the mistakes it keeps on purpose.

Evolution has had a long time to optimise a protein's core. Pick almost any two amino acids that touch each other in a folded structure, swap them for a random pair, and the structure gets worse. That is what it means for a landscape to be funnelled: nearly every local interaction is already close to the best one available at that spot.

Nearly. A minority of contacts are worse than random. They cost the protein stability, and they survive anyway — which is the tell. Something other than folding is paying for them: a binding site that has to open, a hinge that has to move, a catalytic residue held in a strained geometry. These are the frustrated contacts, and they cluster on the parts of a protein that actually do something.

So the measurement is a comparison, not an absolute. Take one contact. Compute its energy as it really is. Then compute what that same spot would have given for a large number of random amino-acid pairs, and ask how far the real one sits from that distribution.

The whole method

Score the native contact. Score the same contact in a few hundred randomised sequences. Report the difference in units of the randomised spread.

That is a Z‑score, and it is the one equation all three tools share.

\[ F_{ij} \;=\; \frac{\left\langle E^{\,\mathrm{decoy}}_{ij}\right\rangle \;-\; E^{\,\mathrm{native}}_{ij}} {\sigma\left(E^{\,\mathrm{decoy}}_{ij}\right)} \]
\(i,\,j\)
the two residues in contact — one contact is one such pair.
\(E^{\,\mathrm{native}}_{ij}\)
the interaction energy of that pair in the real protein, in REU (Rosetta Energy Units, the arbitrary but internally consistent scale REF2015 reports; more negative = more favourable).
\(E^{\,\mathrm{decoy}}_{ij}\)
the same energy after the two residues have been swapped for a random amino-acid pair and the side chains repacked — every side chain re-chosen from Rosetta's library of observed conformations (rotamers) to suit the new sequence, with the backbone held exactly where it was. A decoy is one such randomised sequence; a run uses a few hundred.
\(\left\langle\,\cdot\,\right\rangle,\ \sigma\)
mean and standard deviation across that decoy ensemble. σ is the unit the index is counted in, which is why F is dimensionless: it is a Z‑score, “how many decoy standard deviations”.
\(F_{ij}\)
positive — the native pair beats the random ones: minimally frustrated. Negative — random pairs do better: highly frustrated. Near zero: neutral.
Eq. 1 · Chen et al., Nat Commun 11:5944 (2020) frustx/frustration.py

A sign warning that matters when reading code. The paper writes Eq. 1 upside down, as \((E_0 - \left\langle E_U \right\rangle)\,/\,\sigma\) — \(E_0\) the native (folded) energy, \(E_U\) the unfolded decoy ensemble — which makes a well-optimised contact negative. frustratometeR reports the opposite convention. FrustX follows frustratometeR — positive is good — so that the two are directly comparable, and says so at frustx/frustration.py rather than leaving it to be discovered.

Where they differ

Two symbols carry all the disagreement

Eq. 1 is fixed. What is not fixed is what “E” means — which energy function, and how much of the protein a single contact's energy is allowed to include — and what “decoy” means, i.e. exactly what gets randomised. Those two choices produce three different numbers from the same structure.

Ferreiro / Wolynes, 2007–
frustratometeR

The original, and the field's reference. Energies come from AWSEM, a coarse-grained model: each residue is a few beads, and a contact's energy is a tabulated function of the Cβ–Cβ distance and how buried each residue is. Fast enough to run on anything.

It offers three null models — three different recipes for “what a random version of this contact looks like”, which is the baseline the native is scored against. They randomise genuinely different things, a distinction easy to get wrong from the papers alone.

Atomistic frustration

The same index, moved to a full all-atom force field — Rosetta REF2015. Every side-chain atom is present, so the energy responds to hydrogen-bond geometry, rotamer packing and charge pairing that a bead model cannot represent. The price is that each decoy needs a real side-chain repacking.

Its Eq. 2 defines a contact's energy as the pair term plus half of every other interaction touching either residue. One term is deleted from the force field: fa_rep, the steric repulsion. A random side chain dropped onto a backbone it never evolved for will clash, and that clash is large enough to drown every other signal — so the term is removed from the native and the decoys alike, not from the decoys only.

Why FrustX turns off the paper's Eq. 2

Eq. 2 is the atomistic paper's answer to a real problem: an all-atom force field is many-bodied, so changing one side chain perturbs more than one pair. Its fix is to fold the neighbours in.

\[ E_{ij} \;=\; e_{ij} \;+\; \tfrac{w}{2}\sum_{k \neq j} e_{ik} \;+\; \tfrac{w}{2}\sum_{l \neq i} e_{jl} \]
\(e_{ij}\)
lower-case e is the raw force-field interaction between residues i and j and nothing else. Upper-case \(E_{ij}\) is what gets fed to Eq. 1 — the pair plus its surroundings.
\(\sum_{k \neq j} e_{ik}\)
every other interaction residue i makes, counted at half weight so that no interaction is double-counted when the same formula is applied to its own pair. Likewise \(\sum_{l \neq i} e_{jl}\) for j.
\(w\)
the background weight — FrustX's addition, not the paper's. \(w = 1\) is Eq. 2 exactly as Chen et al. write it; \(w = 0\) strips the background and scores the bare pair. It is a dial rather than a fork because the choice turned out to matter, and the next section is the measurement that set it. CLI flag: --background-weight.
Eq. 2 · Chen et al. 2020, plus FrustX's background weight --background-weight, default 0

Written out, that background is just \(\tfrac{1}{2}(R_i + R_j)\), where \(R_i\) is residue i's total interaction energy with everything around it — a quantity with no reference to j at all. And it dominates: on ubiquitin the direct term is around −0.7 REU against a background near −10 REU. So Eq. 2 as written yields an index that is essentially a residue property wearing a contact's label.

Measured on 1UBQ, sweeping the background weight \(w\) from 0 to 1 over one fixed decoy ensemble, so that every row differs only in how much background is folded in:

Columns 2–5 describe the distribution of \(F_{ij}\) over the 475 contacts. one-body ρ is the Spearman rank correlation — a number between −1 and +1 saying how well one quantity orders another — between the index and the \(c + a_i + a_j\) fit of the next section. Read it as “how much of this index is just residue identity?”; the closer to 1, the less of it is about the contact.

At \(w = 1\) the index finds zero frustrated contacts in ubiquitin and 86% of it is predictable from residue identity alone. At \(w = 0\) the spread, the minimum and the frustrated count all land near frustratometeR's. A frustration index that never reports frustration is not working, so FrustX ships \(w = 0\) and exposes --background-weight for anyone who wants the paper's literal formula.

What a run actually does

Five steps, one of which costs everything

  1. Build the contact map

    Every residue pair whose Cα atoms sit within 10 Å. On ubiquitin that is 475 pairs from 76 residues.

  2. Score the native structure

    Repack the side chains without touching the backbone, then pull the pairwise energy for every contact out of Rosetta's energy graph. The repack matters: comparing an unprepared crystal structure against repacked decoys measures the repacking, not the sequence.

  3. Shuffle and repack, a few hundred times

    Permute the sequence — which preserves amino-acid composition exactly, for free — thread it onto the same fixed backbone, repack, minimise. This is the entire cost of the method. One shuffle yields a decoy energy for every contact at once, so 200 decoys means 200 repacks, not 200 × 475.

  4. Apply Eq. 1

    Per contact, over the decoy ensemble. Pairs whose energy is identically zero in every sequence — two residues in geometric contact with no atomistic interaction — give σ = 0 and are dropped rather than clamped. On ubiquitin that removes 18 of 475.

  5. Split the index

    Fit \(F_{ij} \approx c + a_i + a_j\) by least squares and report both the fit and the residual, so a claim about a pair can be separated from a claim about its two residues.

Decoys are independent, so step 3 forks a worker pool. Measured on 1UBQ: 1.94× at 2 jobs, 3.24× at 4, 4.40× at 8. Sublinearity at 8 is simultaneous multithreading, not a serial bottleneck — the serial part is about 1% of runtime.

Demonstration · ubiquitin

One contact, all the way through

Below is the actual decoy energy distribution for three real contacts in 1UBQ, from 200 shuffled sequences. The histogram is the null. The marked line is the native. Eq. 1 is just the distance between them, counted in standard deviations.

Reading the three cases

LYS27–ASP52 is ubiquitin's buried salt bridge. Its native energy is −4.37 REU while random pairs at that same site average −0.39 ± 0.78. The native beats the null by five standard deviations. Nothing about the geometry is unusual; it is the identities that are doing the work, and swapping them costs real energy.

GLU34–ILE36 is what most of a protein looks like. Native −0.077 against a null mean of −0.078. The real sequence is indistinguishable from a random one here. F = −0.002.

VAL17–LYS29 is the interesting one. Its native energy is positive — +1.64 REU, an actively unfavourable interaction — while the random ensemble sits at −0.26. Ubiquitin is paying about three standard deviations of stability to bury a charged lysine against a valine. That is what a frustrated contact is: a cost the protein carries for a reason that is not folding.

Thresholds, and where they came from

F > 0.78 is called minimally frustrated and F < −1.0 highly frustrated. Both numbers were calibrated for AWSEM, not for REF2015. FrustX inherits them so its output is comparable, and flags in docs/method.md that the −1.0 cut in particular is unvalidated for an atomistic energy function.

The whole protein, and the benchmark

Right distribution, different assignment

Run over all 457 usable contacts, FrustX produces a distribution with the right shape: centred near zero, a long favourable tail, a small minority genuinely frustrated. Those are the same gross statistics frustratometeR reports on the identical input file.

Contact by contact, though, the two tools rank differently. Against frustratometeR's mutational mode — the closest of its three nulls to what FrustX does — the rank correlation over the 344 contacts both tools find is ρ = +0.302. Real, highly significant, and weak.

Why the modes differ so much

The gap between ρ = +0.30 and ρ = +0.13 is not noise, and it is not a bug in either tool. The three frustratometeR modes randomise different things. AWSEM's contact energy depends on three inputs — the Cβ–Cβ distance rij, each residue's burial density (how many neighbours it has, i.e. how buried it is), and the two amino-acid identities — and each mode randomises a different subset of them. Read out of the AWSEM source that builds its bundled binary, not from the papers:

modedistance rijburial densities identitiesenergy scope
configurationalreplacedreplaced randompair only
mutationalnativenative randompair + neighbourhoods
singleresidue—native random, one positionwhole neighbourhood

Configurational mode does not hold geometry fixed. It replaces the pair distance with that of some unrelated contacting pair, then draws the two burial densities from a second, independent random pair — so distance and densities need not even come from the same place in the protein. That is a violent null, and it is why configurational output behaves like a rescaled energy rather than a per-contact Z‑score. This document previously described that null from the papers' prose and got it wrong twice; the version above is read from fix_backbone.cpp.

The largest single cause of the disagreement turned out to be scope, not physics. frustratometeR's mutational energy is not a pair energy at all — it is the pair plus every contact touching either residue. Comparing FrustX's bare pair energy against that was a category mismatch stacked on top of a force-field difference. Matching the scope raises ρ from +0.30 to +0.52 across seven structures, and flips the contact-specific residual from negative to positive in all seven — the first configuration in this project's record with any contact-specific agreement at all.

It is still not the default, for a reason worth stating: the neighbourhood readout is 95% predictable from residue identity. Most of that ρ gain is the two indices becoming jointly degenerate. FrustX exists to give per-contact resolution, and a readout that is 95% one-body does not provide it however well it correlates. --readout neighbourhood is available and documented as a diagnostic.

Two questions, two columns

Is this residue frustrated, or is this pair?

Burial genuinely is most of what determines whether a contact is well-optimised. A deeply buried residue drags all of its contacts in the same direction, and that is a real physical fact, not an artifact — Ferreiro's single-residue frustration is deliberately a residue-level index.

But it means a mostly-additive index cannot support a claim about a particular contact. So rather than choose, FrustX reports both parts of every contact:

\[ F_{ij} \;=\; \underbrace{c + a_i + a_j}_{\text{one-body}} \;+\; \underbrace{s_{ij}}_{\text{contact-specific}} \]
\(a_i\)
one fitted number per residue position, shared by all of that residue's contacts. It absorbs whatever i carries everywhere it goes — burial, exposure, local packing density.
\(c\)
a single intercept for the structure. \(c\) and the \(a_i\) are found by least squares, one dummy variable per position.
\(s_{ij}\)
the residual: what is left once both residues' one-body tendencies are removed. This is the part that belongs to this pair and no other, and it is the column to use when a claim is about a specific contact rather than about a residue.
frustx/additivity.py columns: frustration_index_onebody / _specific

One piece of maths bites here. The design matrix is rank deficient — adding t to c while subtracting t/2 from every ai leaves every fitted value unchanged. So the two per-contact columns are unique and safe, but the per-residue coefficients are identified only up to a constant. Compare them within a run, read differences, never compare across runs.

The residual finds chemistry

The strongest test of whether the split means anything: sort ubiquitin's contacts by the specific part and see what comes up.

Four of the top six are salt bridges — LYS27–ASP52, GLU16–LYS29, GLU51–ARG54, ASP39–ARG74 — and in each the one-body part is close to zero while the specific part carries almost everything. That is exactly the behaviour a contact-specific index should have: a charge pair is well-optimised because those two residues are together, not because either is individually buried.

At the other end, the most frustrated contacts are electrostatic too, in the opposite direction: VAL17–LYS29 (a buried charge with nothing to pair with), GLU24–ASP52 and ASP21–ASN25. This is not a validation of the index's absolute scale, but it is a direct check that the residual is chemistry rather than noise.

One entry in that list is an artifact, and it is left visible on purpose. GLY35–PRO38 scores +6.5 not because anything is optimised but because both residues are nearly atomistically inert, so both the native energy and σ collapse toward zero and their ratio blows up. It is the residual cousin of the σ = 0 contacts dropped at step 4, and any downstream use of this index needs a guard for it.

Standing on

What holds, what failed, what is open

Most of what this project has produced is negative results, and they are more useful than the positive ones — each closed a direction that looked obviously right beforehand.

Holds
The pipeline is exact and reproducible

Rosetta's energy decomposition into pairwise terms is verified exact on real structures, including two terms that are not on the naive energy graph. 85 tests. A pinned-seed run is bit-identical at any job count.

Holds
Agreement with frustratometeR is real but weak

ρ = +0.30 per contact and +0.48 per residue on ubiquitin, extended to 6055 contacts across seven structures with a strikingly consistent pooled ρ = +0.302. Different force field, different null — the two are not expected to be identical, but this is not agreement either.

Holds
Scope mismatch explains more of the gap than physics does

Matching frustratometeR's neighbourhood readout moves ρ +0.30 → +0.52 and flips the contact-specific residual positive in all seven structures. It is the only axis measured so far that moves contact-specific agreement at all.

Withdrawn
“GTPase switch regions become frustrated on activation”

One conformer pair (Rheb) showed it at p = 0.038. Two more pairs chosen for complementary weaknesses did not: Rap2A reversed sign at nominally the same p, and the one resolution-matched pair was flat. Pooled p = 0.86. Withdrawn. The lesson is that the noise floor had been measured over decoy resampling only, which says nothing about crystal-to-crystal variation.

Failed
Generating decoys locally around each contact

The most promising idea on the list, tested properly: every reachable identity pair enumerated exactly rather than sampled, endpoints pre-registered before any index existed. It lost on every one — ρ down, AUC down (area under the ROC curve: how cleanly the index separates the sites an endpoint marks from the ones it does not; 0.5 is a coin flip), and the index became more residue-additive, the opposite of the intent. The predicted 15% variance reduction measured 3%.

Failed
“We were disadvantaged by too few decoys”

Split-half reliability of the index is 0.987 at 500 decoys — that is, score the contacts on one random half of the decoys, again on the other half, and the two rankings agree at 0.987. More decoys buy +0.002 of correlation. Decoy count was never the limiting factor, and neither was the contact-atom definition — switching Cα to Cβ fixes a 74% → ~100% contact-set overlap with frustratometeR without improving agreement at all.

Open
The −1.0 threshold is unvalidated for REF2015

Inherited from AWSEM. The cut point survives a direct test on its own terms, but the resulting category fractions differ from the coarse-grained tool's, and no atomistic calibration exists.

Open
Chen et al. cannot be reproduced contact-by-contact

The paper publishes no per-contact values, its code was never released, the two quantitative systems behind its category ratios are unnamed, and its Fig. 2 “local Q” trace is never defined. Comparison against it is limited to aggregate claims. frustratometeR is the only real benchmark available.

Open
Ligands are not supported

Heteroatoms currently crash the contact map. This is the blocker on everything below.

Where this goes next

From frustration to selectivity

The attraction is obvious. A drug's selectivity problem is that its target has close relatives with near-identical pockets. Sequence identity does not separate them; shape barely does. Frustration is a different kind of coordinate — it asks whether a pocket's local interactions are strained, which is a property that can differ sharply between two pockets that look the same.

Where the EGFR work stands

A companion repository, costbio/egfr_analysis_pipeline_withRosetta, already took this a long way. It extends the atomistic paper's four EGFR–inhibitor complexes to 61 structures and 51 unique ligands, with full ligand parameterisation into Rosetta — the hard, unglamorous part. Structure preparation is done for all 61; frustration has been run on 19, at 50 decoys each.

The headline correlation with affinity is r = −0.297, p = 0.217 — not significant. More important than the p-value is why, because the diagnosis is already in that repository and it is a good one:

Every metric reaching significance is a count, and pocket contact counts range 476–733 across these structures. The significant result is that bigger pockets bind tighter. Dividing pocket size out reverses the sign: frac_minimally goes to +0.35 overall and +0.75 (p = 0.008) on the 11 wild-type non-covalent structures — meaning a less frustrated pocket binds more weakly, opposite to the paper's hypothesis. And the cleanest single case is flat: ligand 03P appears in both 3POZ (23 pM) and 3W2O (8400 pM), a 365‑fold affinity difference, with frac_minimally of 0.5007 and 0.4964.

Three things have to change before this can work

  1. Stop predicting affinity. Predict differences.

    Absolute binding affinity is the wrong endpoint and always was. It mixes Kd (a direct binding constant) with IC50 (the concentration that halves an enzyme's activity) across labs — two assays that do not share a scale — it is dominated by ligand size and lipophilicity, and no local structural coordinate is going to beat a docking score at it. Selectivity is a paired quantity — the same ligand against two targets — and the pairing cancels exactly the systematics that are drowning the current signal. This is the single largest available improvement and it costs no new compute, only a different table.

  2. Use the contact-specific column, not the raw index.

    This is where the additivity work pays off. Pocket frustration is dominated by burial, and burial is nearly identical across the ATP sites of a kinase family — so the one-body part is close to constant and contributes almost nothing to a selectivity contrast while contributing most of the variance. The residual is where a selectivity-determining residue difference such as T790M — the EGFR gatekeeper substitution behind most first-generation inhibitor resistance — would have to show up. A contrast built on frustration_index_specific starts with a far better signal-to-noise ratio than one built on the index.

  3. Define a null for protein–ligand contacts.

    The blocker. FrustX shuffles a sequence; a ligand has no sequence, so there is nothing to permute on that side of the contact. The defensible null is to randomise the protein residue only and leave the ligand fixed — which is structurally frustratometeR's single-residue null, and FrustX does not implement it. It has to come before ligands are wired in, or the numbers will not mean anything. Heteroatom support in the contact map is the smaller half of this job.

What a real test would look like

The dataset that fits this design already exists: published kinase profiling panels give one inhibitor's dissociation constant against a few hundred kinases. That turns a 19-point regression with mixed assay types into hundreds of within-ligand contrasts, each of which cancels the ligand entirely. The prediction to test is specific and falsifiable — the kinase whose pocket contacts are more frustrated in the apo (ligand-free) state is the one the inhibitor binds more weakly — and it fails cleanly if the contrast comes out flat.

Two honest caveats to carry in. Frustration is computed on a fixed backbone, so it cannot see induced fit, which is a large part of real kinase selectivity. And covalent inhibitors — 15 of the 61 EGFR structures — are outside the model entirely: their selectivity comes from a reactive cysteine, not from any equilibrium energy this index describes. They should be analysed separately or excluded, not pooled.

The shortest path

Finish the 42 remaining EGFR structures at matched decoy count, re-express every endpoint as a within-ligand contrast on the contact-specific column, and only then decide whether protein–ligand contacts are worth the work of a new null.

Two things to check before trusting a run

How many contacts were dropped for σ = 0 — a large fraction means the contact map is admitting pairs with no atomistic interaction, and --contact-atom CB is the fix. And additive_r2 in run.json: if it is high, read the specific column rather than the index before making any claim about an individual contact.
